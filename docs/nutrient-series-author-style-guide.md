# Nutrient Series Author Style Guide

This guide governs the second content line: general education articles about nutrient intake, food-pattern clues, label reading, and when to ask a licensed professional.

The series must never diagnose deficiency, map symptoms to a nutrient, recommend supplements, give dosage advice, name products or brands, or imply that an article can decide what a reader should take, buy, stop, or replace.

## Shared Safety Rules

- Use "may be worth discussing" rather than "means you are low in".
- Use "food pattern", "intake pattern", "label record", and "open question" rather than diagnosis language.
- Do not create symptom checkers.
- Do not list symptoms as proof of deficiency.
- Do not recommend supplement forms, brands, stores, products, or dosages.
- Keep personal factors separate: pregnancy, breastfeeding, medication use, allergies, surgery, eating disorder history, medical conditions, and personal nutrition plans.
- End each article with the required wording: "This article is general education only and is not medical advice."

## Author Roster

Each author owns 10 articles in the 50-article nutrient series. Use the same site standards, but vary rhythm, transitions, sentence length, and preferred framing.

### Mara Lin

- Role: Label systems editor.
- Temperament: ordered, steady, table-friendly.
- Best for: nutrients with label math, Daily Value, panel fields, fortified foods, and comparison notes.
- Sentence pattern: medium length, careful sequencing.
- Common moves: "Start with the field you can verify", "Keep the unit attached", "Use one row for..."
- Avoid: cozy intros, broad reassurance, dramatic metaphors.

Sample paragraph:

> Start with the label field that can be checked. Write the nutrient name, amount, unit, serving size, and percent Daily Value before adding any interpretation. If one of those fields is missing, the note should say that clearly.

### Nora Vale

- Role: Everyday routines writer.
- Temperament: warm, practical, unhurried.
- Best for: grocery patterns, weekly food logs, routine check-ins, and gentle follow-up questions.
- Sentence pattern: slightly softer, with a human pause.
- Common moves: "It helps to notice", "You do not have to decide today", "A small record is enough."
- Avoid: over-soothing, therapy voice, vague comfort.

Sample paragraph:

> It helps to look at a few ordinary meals before trying to explain anything. A short food note from the week can show whether a nutrient rarely appears, appears sometimes, or is hard to track from labels alone.

### Eli Brooks

- Role: Checklist editor.
- Temperament: direct, clipped, concrete.
- Best for: quick checks, "what to copy" articles, compact decision boundaries, and recurring templates.
- Sentence pattern: short. Active. Minimal setup.
- Common moves: "Write down...", "Do not guess", "Use a separate line for..."
- Avoid: long background sections, abstract transitions, soft filler.

Sample paragraph:

> Write the serving size first. Then write the nutrient amount and unit. If percent Daily Value appears, copy it. If it does not appear, write "not shown." Do not fill the gap from memory.

### June Carter

- Role: Claims and wording editor.
- Temperament: precise, cautious, language-first.
- Best for: "supports", "rich in", "excellent source", nutrient content claims, and risky wording.
- Sentence pattern: polished, exact, editorial.
- Common moves: "The wording matters", "Do not upgrade the claim", "Copy the phrase before interpreting it."
- Avoid: sounding legalistic, overexplaining statutes, making the tone stiff.

Sample paragraph:

> The phrase matters. "Contains" is not the same as "high in," and neither phrase answers whether the nutrient is personally relevant. Copy the claim first, then check the panel field that the claim points toward.

### Theo Grant

- Role: Comparison notes editor.
- Temperament: practical, source-aware, note-sheet driven.
- Best for: side-by-side comparisons, product pages, cost and serving records, and "what remains unknown."
- Sentence pattern: pragmatic, with concrete note fields.
- Common moves: "Put this in its own row", "Keep the source visible", "Leave the question open."
- Avoid: sounding like a spreadsheet manual, making product fit conclusions.

Sample paragraph:

> Put the source in the first row: label photo, package panel, product page, or meal note. Then write the date checked. A note without a source can still be useful today, but it is harder to trust later.

## Anti-AI Polish Checks

Before any article moves to external review, remove or reduce:

- Repeated "calm", "neutral", "context", "navigate", "empower", "journey", "unlock".
- Template openings that begin the same way across articles.
- Over-balanced paragraphs where every section has exactly two similar sentences.
- Generic disclaimers repeated in the same rhythm.
- Overuse of "important", "helpful", "simple", and "overall".

Keep:

- Specific note fields.
- Plain verbs.
- Small asymmetry in section length.
- Human transitions that fit the assigned author.
- Safety language that is direct but not mechanical.

## Review Prompt Addition

For the nutrient series, add this instruction to ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude review prompts:

> Also judge whether the assigned author voice is visible without becoming gimmicky. Flag AI-like sameness, template rhythm, over-polished phrasing, or generic wellness language as an editorial issue. Do not suggest adding brands, products, affiliate examples, medical advice, symptom checkers, or supplement recommendations.
